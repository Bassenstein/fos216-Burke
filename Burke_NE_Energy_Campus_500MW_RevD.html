Executive Summary

The most ambitious
lakefront proposal
in American history

Every previous Burke proposal started by trying to close the airport — the single hardest, slowest regulatory gate in Cleveland. This proposal does the opposite: the airport stays open and the main runway keeps flying. The energy campus is built entirely on the ±150-acre dredge-disposal peninsula on the northeast corner of Burke — the confined-disposal fill east of the East Basin that is already closed to the public and unusable for aviation. Vehicles reach it through a single secured gateway on the southeast side, and a continuous security wall, vegetated buffer, and patrol road separate the campus from the active airfield to the south, satisfying FAA Part 139 air-operations-area separation. The campus is anchored by a 500 MW hyperscale data center — expandable to 1 GW — powered by an on-site GE Vernova 7HA turbine power island with exhaust-heat steam recovery, and it still turns Lake Erie water into green hydrogen and green ammonia while layering civic space and a full educational pipeline on top.

"The breakthrough is that no airport closure is required at all. Cleveland keeps Burke flying and builds a nationally significant energy and data-center campus on land the runway never touches — the closed dredge-disposal peninsula on the northeast corner. The hardest political fight simply disappears." — Burke NE Energy Campus Concept, 2026

The campus integrates seven converging pillars: a 500 MW hyperscale data center (reserved to grow to 1 GW) cooled by Lake Erie deep water; an on-site GE Vernova 7HA gas-turbine power island whose turbine exhaust is captured by heat-recovery steam generators to make steam for campus hot water and district heating, and which is engineered to co-fire the campus's own green hydrogen; a reserved, pre-permitted footprint for a small modular reactor (SMR) that lifts the campus to 1 GW of firm, zero-carbon baseload; Lake Erie water purification and PEM electrolysis producing green hydrogen; Sierra Energy FastOx gasification converting Cleveland MSW into syngas; Haber-Bosch green ammonia synthesis; and active discharge reoxygenation returning expelled water to full ecological health — all behind a hardened security wall that keeps the campus and the airfield completely separate. At full operation the campus supports 1,200+ permanent high-quality jobs and generates roughly $1.2B/year in gross revenue, anchored by the data center.

Campus Master Plan

Twelve zones.
One secured
NE-corner campus.

The campus occupies only the ±150-acre dredge-disposal peninsula on Burke's northeast corner — the confined-disposal fill east of the East Basin. The 6,604 ft primary runway and all active airfield land to the south are untouched and keep operating. Because the fill is engineered dredge material, every heavy structure sits on driven piles to bearing strata. A continuous 12-ft security wall, a 100-ft vegetated buffer, and a perimeter patrol road run along the entire southern boundary, separating the campus from the airfield's air-operations area per FAA Part 139; the only vehicular entry is a hardened secured gateway on the southeast side off N. Marginal Road. A reserved interior parcel is pre-graded for a small modular reactor. City retains fee ownership via 99-year ground lease.

At 500 MW electrolyser input, water consumption is ~3.6M litres/day — trivial against Lake Erie's 25,700 km² surface. The intake station doubles as a Great Lakes Water Quality monitoring hub partnered with Cleveland Water Alliance and NOAA's Great Lakes Environmental Research Laboratory.

Zone 3 · Phase 2 · 2030–2032

Sierra Energy FastOx Gasification

FastOx shaft gasification — derived from Kaiser Steel blast furnace technology by Sierra Energy (Davis, CA) — converts Cleveland MSW at temperatures exceeding 2,200°C using oxygen and steam injection. No preprocessing required. Waste descends through drying, pyrolysis, gasification, and partial oxidation zones producing clean syngas (H₂ + CO) and inert vitrified slag suitable for road aggregate. The syngas feeds the Haber-Bosch ammonia synthesis loop after water-gas shift reaction and CO₂ removal. Cleveland pays gate fees to tip waste at the campus instead of landfill, converting a $15–20M/year cost center into a revenue stream. Zero liquid discharge design.

Zone 4 · Phase 1 · 2029–2031 · SMR-ready

GE Vernova 7HA Power Island

The campus's primary generation and the data center's dedicated power: GE Vernova 7HA-class gas turbines in 1×1 combined-cycle blocks delivering approximately 500 MW net. Turbine exhaust (≈630°C) is captured by heat-recovery steam generators (HRSGs); the steam drives a steam turbine for additional output and bleeds process steam for campus hot water and district heating — a true combined-heat-and-power island rather than simple-cycle waste. The 7HA is rated to co-fire hydrogen, so the campus's own green H₂ progressively displaces natural gas. A reserved, pre-graded interior parcel and switchyard headroom let a small modular reactor add firm zero-carbon baseload and lift the site to 1 GW. Backup and island resilience come from a fuel-flexible generation fleet — Mainspring Energy linear generators paired with Hyliion KARNO modules, plus BESS — sized N+1 so the campus rides through any grid or turbine event. Mainspring's units are shipping commercially today and anchor near-term availability; KARNO scales into the fleet as it reaches volume. Both run on natural gas now and the campus's green hydrogen as it ramps.

Zone 5 · Phase 2 · 2030–2032

Haber-Bosch Green Ammonia Synthesis

1,200 t/day green ammonia plant combining electrolytic H₂ with nitrogen from an on-site Air Separation Unit. Synthesis loop operates at 150–300 bar, 400–500°C over iron catalyst with K₂O and Al₂O₃ promoters. Only 15–25% converts per pass; unreacted gas recycles. Ammonia serves three simultaneous markets: zero-carbon fertilizer for Great Lakes agriculture, hydrogen carrier for marine bunkering (cracked back to H₂ at point of use), and pipeline export to the NE Ohio industrial corridor. The FastOx syngas-to-ammonia integration creates a circular economy: Cleveland MSW becomes fertilizer. Truck loading bay is the most safety-critical zone — IDLH 300 ppm NH₃, MC-331 tanker specifications, vapor recovery, emergency shutdown.

The Core Process

Lake Erie water
to green hydrogen
in six stages

1
Stage 1 · Intake

Lake Erie water draw

A submerged intake crib positioned 300–600 metres offshore draws water below the thermal stratification layer — consistently 38–55°F year-round regardless of surface ice or summer warming. Fish exclusion screens at mesh 1 mm, approach velocity controlled to under 0.15 m/s, meet Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement (GLWQA) requirements non-negotiably. Raw intake capacity: 40,000 gallons per minute feeding the shoreline pump station. At 500 MW electrolyser load, water consumption is approximately 3.6 million litres per day — a trivial draw on a lake with 25,700 km² of surface area.

2
Stage 2 · Water Treatment

Purification to electrolysis grade

The treatment train achieves electrolysis-grade feedwater in three stages: (1) Softening via ion exchange removes calcium, magnesium, and other hardness ions that would foul PEM membranes; (2) Reverse osmosis reduces TDS by 95%+ and removes particulates; (3) Final deionisation (DI) polishing achieves <1 µS/cm conductivity — the specification required by PEM electrolyser manufacturers to protect the proton exchange membrane and avoid accelerated stack degradation. This ultra-pure water is what makes the electrolyser farm's claimed 80,000-hour stack life achievable.

3
Stage 3 · Electrolysis

Splitting water with electricity

500 MW of PEM electrolyser current passes through the purified water: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂. The proton exchange membrane allows only protons (H⁺) to pass, producing high-purity hydrogen at the cathode. At 50 kWh/kg system efficiency, 500 MW produces approximately 10,000 kg of hydrogen per hour — 180 tonnes per day. PEM output H₂ is approximately 99.9% pure, at 30–50 bar, containing trace moisture. Stack lifetime at rated current density: approximately 80,000 hours (about 9 years) before major stack replacement, a primary O&M cost driver.

4
Stage 4 · Purification & Drying

H₂ conditioning for use and storage

PEM output hydrogen contains trace moisture and <0.1% oxygen. A pressure swing adsorption (PSA) drier or membrane drier reduces dewpoint below −40°C, protecting high-pressure storage vessels from internal corrosion. A catalytic deoxo unit (palladium catalyst) removes O₂ to <1 ppm where required by storage specifications. For direct turbine co-fire supply, the deoxo step is often omitted. Final purity: 99.95%+ H₂, dry, at 30–50 bar — ready for buffer storage, the 7HA fuel skid, or compression to 350 bar for tube storage and transport.

5
Stage 5 · Buffer Storage

Operational decoupling and flexibility

A hydrogen buffer decouples the electrolyser schedule from turbine demand: it absorbs electrolyser output during low-load periods and steadies H₂ supply to the 7HA co-fire skid during transients, while the Mainspring + KARNO backup fleet covers black-start and momentary island events. The 72-hour bulk storage field (Zone 9) provides the strategic buffer for grid services, demand response, and raising the turbines' hydrogen co-fire fraction on demand.

6
Stage 6 · Power & Heat

The 7HA power island: fuel to electricity + steam

The GE Vernova 7HA turbines generate the campus's electricity, burning natural gas at commissioning and progressively co-firing the green hydrogen produced upstream. Rather than vent the ≈630°C exhaust, heat-recovery steam generators capture it to raise steam — driving a bottoming steam turbine for extra megawatts and bleeding process steam for campus hot water and district heating. That combined-heat-and-power recovery is what lifts overall fuel efficiency well past a simple-cycle plant. Every added kilogram of on-site H₂ co-fire lowers the carbon intensity of the whole campus, and the reserved SMR parcel offers a firm zero-carbon path to 1 GW.

The Burke campus creates a five-tier educational pipeline connecting every level of Cleveland's academic ecosystem to real careers in clean energy — and uniquely, every tier uses the Burke campus itself as a live classroom. No simulation. No approximation. Real industrial hydrogen infrastructure at real operating scale.

Tier 1 · Elementary
Cleveland Public Schools

Early Awareness & Environmental Literacy

Field trips from Cleveland Metropolitan School District (CMSD) elementary schools to the Energy Discovery Centre — the glass-floored observation deck above the electrolyser farm. Students in grades 3–6 watch hydrogen being made from lake water in real time, then trace it into the 7HA power island, where it is co-fired to make the electricity powering the lights in the building they're standing in. Curriculum modules co-developed with CMSD science teachers aligned to Ohio science standards.

Tier 2 · High School
CMSD · MC² STEM · John Hay

Two Pathways: Technician Track & Engineering Track

MC² STEM High School, John Hay STEM, and Early College High School are natural feeders. The campus creates two distinct pathways:

Technician Pathway: Junior technician apprenticeships for 10th–12th graders — 10–15 paid hours per week on-site. Students rotate through water treatment operations, electrolyser monitoring, turbine, Mainspring/KARNO genset, and BESS module checks, and control-room data logging. These are entry-level positions with union-negotiated wage scale, structured mentorship from licensed plant operators, and a guaranteed Tri-C interview upon graduation. Burke becomes one of the SWAG program's anchor employers.

Engineering Pathway: AP Chemistry, Physics, and Environmental Science co-taught by CMSD teachers and CWRU faculty using Burke plant data as primary source material. A student's AP Chemistry lab assignment might be analysing the purity of hydrogen output from the electrolyser. Dual enrolment via Ohio's College Credit Plus earns Tri-C credit for courses taken on or near Burke.

Policy & Federal Strategy

A far more
compelling
federal ask

Every previous Burke proposal asked Congress and the FAA to close the airport — the slowest, least certain approval in the entire project. This plan removes that ask entirely. By building only on the closed NE dredge-disposal peninsula and keeping the main runway and Part 139 operations intact, the campus sidesteps the closure fight while still qualifying for the same federal clean-energy programs — now joined by data-center/AI and advanced-nuclear (SMR) pathways.
